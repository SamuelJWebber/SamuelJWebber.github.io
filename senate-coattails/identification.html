The first validity test · actual result

This pilot does not yet support a causal claim.

The fixed five-point local-linear comparison produces a −10.90-point jump in the party’s previous presidential vote share (95% interval: −19.21 to −2.59). A nomination today cannot cause votes cast years earlier. The selected sample has a serious baseline imbalance; a close margin alone has not made these cases comparable.

These are 33 provisional primary contests in 20 states; official vote counts have been checked for 25. The diagnosis uses strictly prior MIT presidential returns, not the source’s ambiguous lagged-election fields. No current teammate outcomes were fitted in this archived money-based pilot.

Fixed-window sensitivity and precision

Two-point window, difference in means: −5.26 points [−11.03, +0.51], 15 contests. Ten-point window, local linear: −3.51 [−9.08, +2.06], 65 contests. A wider interval crossing zero does not establish comparability. The main local-linear fit differs from a raw difference of group means.

The actual five-point design has hypothetical detection thresholds of about 7–12 points when residual outcome variation is assumed to be 3–5 points. These are planning scenarios, not measured coattails or achieved power. More House districts do not add independent nomination decisions.

What changes in this approach

The nomination supplies the comparison.

Donor support defines the candidate contrast. It does not, by itself, establish a causal effect.

01

Measure support before the primary

Count distinct identifiable donors for both contenders using the same pre-primary cutoff. Compare them within the same party, state and race. The population denominator is identical, so it cancels from their comparison.

The archived pilot below uses pre-primary money shares. The newly executed study uses distinct disclosed donor counts and fits current teammate outcomes.

02

Use the nomination threshold

Compare close races where the higher-support contender narrowly wins with those where that contender narrowly loses. Near a tie, underlying party strength should vary smoothly while the identity of the nominee switches.

This assumption needs evidence: prior vote balance, both candidates’ characteristics, valid primary rules and sufficient independent contests.

03

Count teammates’ votes

Measure House and governor vote shares and votes per pre-election electorate. Keep turnout inside the effect: a candidate can bring voters out, change their choices, or do both.

Report the nominee’s own result separately. A stronger fundraising base is not automatically greater general-election appeal.

The question this can answer

What happens to the rest of the ticket when a party narrowly nominates the candidate with broader pre-primary support, instead of the actual rival? This is the effect of that candidate and campaign together. It is not the isolated effect of charisma or an estimate of Ossoff’s personal contribution.

Prior elections are a check.

A previous party surge should not jump at the nomination threshold. Compare earlier presidential and teammate results on both sides. Earlier coattails may remain in those results; they are a diagnostic of comparability, not the source of identification.

A close race is not enough.

Small samples can be badly imbalanced by chance. Report the precision the sample can support, keep the bandwidth fixed before looking at teammate effects, and do not manufacture more independent experiments by counting every House district separately.

Executed design · outside this dashboard

The Comey letter and early voting

Kayleigh McCrary’s October 2026 working paper uses the unexpected letter and pre-existing early-voting rules, comparing 2016 with 2012. It reports 0.79 points of down-ballot movement per 1 point of presidential movement and separately examines a turnout channel.

That is the author’s estimate for a particular presidential shock. It depends on comparable trends across states and is not a universal candidate multiplier. The dashboard has not independently replicated it.

A warning worth building into the design

Check what happened before the primary.

Hall and Thompson’s 2025 correction found substantial prior presidential-vote imbalance in an earlier close-primary study. They withdrew reliance on its large turnout findings.

The lesson is practical: inspect balance before interpreting a close nomination as random. Adding controls after discovering imbalance does not automatically repair the design.

We also checked a statewide shock. In the 2012 Cooperative Congressional Election Study, the two weeks around Richard Mourdock’s October 23 remark contain 459 registered Indiana respondents interviewed before and only 74 after, excluding the event day and interviews crossing it. That is too small for a precise one- or two-point effect. Interview timing measures intentions at different times; it does not prevent earlier interviewees from hearing the news before voting. This survey cannot simply be relabeled a turnout experiment.

Sources, measurement and the original design

Primary data. Myers’s replication data supply a selected sample of contested primary races with ideology measures. Algara and coauthors’ candidate data supply names, nomination outcomes and pre-primary money shares. This is not the full universe of Senate primaries.

From design to execution. The new donor study links both contenders, applies election-specific pre-primary cutoffs, fixes the specifications, and reports nominee and teammate outcomes alongside balance checks. Historical filing vintages and sample selection remain limitations. Missing donor records remain missing, not zero.

What “all-star” would mean. Broader donor support is a testable operational definition, not a claim about inherent talent. A donor-based contrast and a separately validated measure of general-election appeal should remain distinct. Do not choose the definition because it produces a large teammate effect.

The total effect. Current turnout and campaign spending may carry the candidate’s influence, so they are not adjustment variables for the total-effect question. Votes per electorate, vote share and ballot completion describe different aspects; aggregate returns do not uniquely separate persuasion from mobilization.

The causal claim. Even a valid nomination discontinuity applies to close contests with these types of rivals. It does not identify the effect of replacing any ordinary candidate with a once-in-a-generation star. Dividing the teammate effect by the nominee’s own effect does not automatically create a causal coattail multiplier.
