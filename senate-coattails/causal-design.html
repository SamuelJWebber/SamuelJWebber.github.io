Toward a causal test

Change the nominee.
Measure the ticket.

The question is what teammates gain or lose when a party nominates its stronger candidate. A better candidate measure and a credible nomination experiment are two separate requirements.

By Samuel Webber ↗

The ideal experiment

Randomly assign the nominee.
Let the campaign happen.

Hold the initial election setting and teammate roster fixed. Let both nominees mobilize, persuade, raise money and provoke responses. Count what changes for every teammate.

The assignment unit is a statewide nomination—not an individual voter or a precinct.

The closest historical approximation

Measure appeal first.
Use a close nomination second.

The ideal replacement experiment cannot be recreated within one completed statewide election. Close nominations offer a testable approximation across elections.

01 · Measurement

Compare real alternatives

Find a pre-nomination survey that asks about candidate A against opponent X, and candidate B against the same X. The difference measures comparative general-election appeal under a common survey, date and opponent.

02 · Assignment

Find the decisive near-tie

Compare elections where the higher-appeal contender barely won with those where they barely lost. Use the round that actually determined the nomination, including final runoffs.

03 · Outcome

Count teammates’ votes

Measure same-party House and concurrent governor outcomes. Report vote share and votes per fixed pre-campaign population. Allow both turnout and persuasion to contribute.

The proposed estimand

The effect of nominating the candidate with greater measured pre-primary general-election appeal instead of their actual rival, among close nominations. This is the candidate-and-campaign package; it does not isolate innate charisma.

Why not just subtract last election?

A state’s political trajectory can affect candidate recruitment, donations and teammate results together. Prior elections are balance checks and precision controls. The causal claim must come from the nomination assignment, not the subtraction.

What close does—and does not—establish

A narrow margin is a starting point. Party composition, earlier election results, institutions and sorting around the cutoff still need scrutiny. More precinct observations do not create more independent nomination experiments.

Preserve the turnout channel

Freeze the population denominator before the campaign. Count teammate votes, registration and turnout where available. Citizen voting-age population is an approximation, not the exact legally eligible electorate. A fixed registered-voter cohort would miss newly induced registrations.

Do not remove the effect we want

Do not control for postnomination turnout, fundraising, campaign spending or the nominee’s own general-election result. These may be mechanisms through which the nominee helps—or hurts—the ticket.

A noncircular role for donations

Train the proxy on appeal.
Test coattails separately.

Candidate quality is the input. Teammates’ election results remain the outcome to explain.

Proposed prediction target

The within-survey
candidate appeal gap.

Use early donor breadth, in-state breadth, repeat giving and growth to predict the difference between two real contenders against a common opponent. Hold out whole contests and later election cycles. Keep every candidate and poll from one contest in the same fold.

Donor measurement must use information available before the nomination. Per-capita scaling alone cannot change the ranking of two candidates in the same state.

Separate causal analysis

Lock the score.
Then compare close nominations.

After out-of-time validation, use the frozen model to score unpolled contenders. Report directly polled and model-imputed comparisons separately, carrying measurement uncertainty. Predictive success does not establish the nomination experiment’s validity.

No new donor-to-poll model has been fitted in this audit. Weak prediction would leave a donor-breadth treatment, not a validated candidate-quality treatment.

Identification

A defensible threshold comparison

Audit decisive rounds, dates and institutions. Examine pretreatment balance and sorting. Use a complete roster before filtering for data availability. Keep the treatment definition fixed rather than selecting the specification that makes teammates improve.

Precision

Statewide assignments are scarce

The prior House estimate’s standard error is about 3.36 points. Its rough 80%-power detectable effect is near ten points. Fourteen possible additions cannot promise a precise one- or two-point answer.

Scope

Close-primary candidates are local evidence

This design concerns nomination among realistic rivals. It does not identify a superstar’s whole career, earlier primary mobilization or the individual effect of a candidate who wins their primary easily.

Why other plausible experiments answer different questions

Randomized campaign exposure can capture both persuasion and turnout when its outcomes include nonvoters and aggregate teammate votes. It measures the incremental campaign intervention, however, rather than replacing the statewide nominee.

Unexpected scandals or other shocks can identify how a particular event affects a ticket when exposure and the comparison group are credible. A simple comparison of early and late voters is confounded by who chooses each voting time. Voting records also do not generally attach a candidate choice to an exact voting date.

Cast-vote records show ticket splitting and ballot roll-off. They do not supply the missing counterfactual or include people who stayed home. Millions of ballots do not solve a shortage of independent candidate assignments.

Current conclusion

We have a better candidate-appeal measure, verified missing nomination events and a concrete data map. We do not yet have a sufficiently populated, validated design that establishes the causal all-star effect. The existing donor study remains inconclusive.
