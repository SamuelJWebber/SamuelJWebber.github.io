The research question / Same-ballot effects

Do all-star candidates lift the ticket?

First define candidate strength independently of teammates’ results. Freeze that definition. Then measure how the party’s House and governor candidates perform when a strong Senate candidate appears on the same ballot.

What this measures, and what it cannot establish

The desired effect is the change in other candidates’ results caused by putting an unusually effective candidate on the ballot. A useful predictor of Senate performance does not, by itself, create a causal comparison for the rest of the ticket. State-specific political shifts, opponent weakness and candidate selection can affect both.

The same-ballot models control prior party performance, teammate and Senate incumbency, state and election-year effects, and score availability. They do not control current turnout, current campaign spending or the current Senate result. Vote share can reflect both turnout and persuasion; it does not count the total number of additional voters.

Prior House and governor results may already contain earlier coattails. Adjusting for them estimates the incremental current association beyond established support. The on/off-ballot analysis instead freezes a senator’s measured strength at the start of a term and compares the intervening House elections with the next scheduled Senate election. Its placebo checks whether a similar trend was already under way.

The chronology is explicit: model choices use development elections, later own-race elections evaluate those choices, and each score used for teammate analysis is fitted from earlier elections. Earlier analyses were already known when this extension was specified. This is a retrospective research project, not a preregistered natural experiment. Intervals are conditional on the fitted strength measure and do not cover all model-selection or measurement uncertainty.

Held-out check: does fixed Senate strength help predict the other races?

This tests predictive transport to teammates. It does not choose or retune the all-star definition. The baseline and augmented model use the same rows and fitting period; lower error is better.

What exactly is frozen, and who remains unmeasured?

The validated primary measure is the Democratic nominee’s prior Senate strength minus the Republican nominee’s prior strength, with separate indicators for whether each historical result is observed. The continuous comparison is the primary statistical test. Top-decile status is a fixed secondary way to inspect exceptional candidates; its cutoff is the 90th percentile of 371 observed nominee-history appearances in 1982–2008, with repeat appearances included.

The star model compares Democratic and Republican star presence symmetrically and includes a separate lower-decile indicator. The lower cutoff is −3.66 points, which is not a definition of an “awful” candidate. Missing histories are unscored, not weak. Prior performance includes opponents, local conditions and noise; it does not establish intrinsic charisma. A statewide breakout candidate without earlier Senate history is outside this definition.

House comparisons require the same district boundaries in the previous election. The source’s 2022 redistricting flags are incomplete, so matched geography is checked separately. Governor comparisons use the previous regular statewide election and audit missing returns, special elections and incumbency. The download reports every exclusion and sample period.

Earlier exploratory coattail results were already known when this extension was designed. These are retrospective analyses with specified fitting holdouts, not a prospectively preregistered test. The strength measure is not changed to obtain a desired spillover estimate.

A causal extension would need a source of plausibly random variation in whether the independently rated stronger candidate appears on the ballot—for example, enough near-tied primaries with scores fixed before those primaries. Such an assignment design has not yet been established here.

How the tests prevent hindsight

The main Senate models train through 2008, compare on 2010–2014 development elections, test on 2016–2022, and run a separate 2024 confirmation. Models are refitted only on earlier elections. The development comparison selects the broad-coverage default; the later outcomes do not choose its specification. Candidate-disjoint and state-disjoint checks remove those identities from fitting. Earlier known results remain usable historical information.

Features use earlier presidential votes, incumbency and the White House party known before Election Day. Current-election national results, final race margins, eventual presidencies and today’s view counts are excluded. No race is removed merely for having a very lopsided vote share. Endorsements reconstructed using the full historical sample and digital-ad archives assembled after Election Day are labeled retrospective. Other datasets have their own stated splits.

These are retrospective backtests run in November 2026, not forecasts that were issued before the elections. The splits were fixed for this analysis before fitting. Coverage limitations and modeling decisions still matter, even when the numerical holdout is untouched.

The 2026 view is a held-out test, not a prospective one. The model, its features and its sample rules were fixed on 2026-11-05 from 1982–2024 data only, two days after Election Day. No 2026 vote was used to fit or select it, and the scoring protocol and every 2026 prediction were committed before any 2026 error was computed. It is not a forecast issued before the election, and the votes are unofficial counts until each state certifies.

What researchers already measure

Talent as a campaigner

A compelling candidate, a competent officeholder and a well-funded campaign are related but distinct. Research offers more targeted measures than a large previous winning margin. None alone identifies how many votes a candidate delivers to teammates.

An existing quasi-experimental approach

Marc Meredith uses candidates’ home-area advantage to test whether personal support for a governor carries over to other statewide candidates, accounting for county and state-election differences. He estimates 0.1–0.2 extra points for the party’s secretary-of-state or attorney-general nominee per extra point of gubernatorial personal support. The result can include turnout and persuasion; it does not separate them. This is a local-appeal estimate, not a statewide estimate for any particular star. APSR study ↗

The Senate version is feasible to investigate: Hunt documents home-area advantages in Senate elections, 1968–2018. Combining those geographic ties with governor and House returns could test spillovers, subject to the assumption that those ties do not independently favor the rest of the ticket. It would need earlier-election placebos and evidence that a Senate home advantage actually exists in the selected sample. Senate study ↗

Donor breadth · measurement design

Can donor breadth identify an all-star?

Yes, it is a useful hypothesis to test. Count people who support the candidate before voting, then ask whether that support is unusually broad given the race. A fundraising score measures enthusiasm and campaign reach; electoral appeal still needs independent validation.

01 · Local support

Unique in-state donors

Count each person once in a fixed early window, per 10,000 eligible voters. Ten recurring gifts from one person remain one donor. Track first-time political donors and geographic breadth as secondary measures when comparable histories are available.

02 · Fair comparison

Actual ÷ expected

Predict donor participation for the same office, party and cycle using population, incumbency, campaign age, earlier donor participation and underlying race competitiveness. Fit on other elections, then rank candidates by support above expectation. Keep out-of-state donors separate.

03 · Independent test

Does support travel?

Validate the score against held-out personal overperformance and pre-election polling of alternative nominees facing the same opponent. For coattails, freeze the score before a primary and compare close contests where the higher-scoring nominee barely wins or loses.

Control for the race before the candidate changes it

Final margins and late polls partly reflect candidate strength. Use earlier partisan lean and opponent incumbency for baseline competitiveness; show poll-adjusted scores separately. The primary design would estimate the total effect of nominating a donor-popular candidate, including money and organization. Close statewide primaries may be too scarce for precise estimates.

The small-donor data are usable, with gaps

Campaign filings generally identify donors once their cycle total exceeds $200. ActBlue’s federal conduit filings itemize even smaller gifts, so those records can recover many otherwise hidden donors. Match the ultimate recipient, reconcile amendments, and avoid counting conduit and campaign records twice. Direct unitemized gifts still leave missing people; the standard FEC individual-contributions bulk file is not a complete small-donor dataset.

Case study · Georgia 2026

How much did Ossoff help Bottoms?

Jon Ossoff appeared in an ad for Keisha Lance Bottoms and directed $3.6 million to Georgia’s coordinated Democratic campaign. Both won. To measure how much he helped, we’d need to know how she would have performed against Rick Jackson with another Democrat running for Senate.

The target is the full effect of having Ossoff on the ballot: persuasion, turnout, fundraising, organization and any opposing-party mobilization. A study of a single endorsement ad would identify only that intervention’s added effect. It would not answer the full candidacy question.

A broader cross-election test could compare near-tied primaries where a candidate rated as a stronger campaigner before the primary barely won or barely lost. Follow the other same-party nominees’ votes and net vote margins per eligible voter, alongside turnout and vote share. This needs enough comparable primaries and credible balance around the threshold; it would not by itself identify Ossoff’s individual contribution.

About the study

Methods & sources

Download study ↓

What counts as a coattail?

Extra votes a candidate brings to another nominee from the same party. These estimates are conditional historical associations. Candidate selection, opponents and changing state party strength can influence both races. Their intervals quantify sampling uncertainty, not uncertainty about unmeasured confounding.

How candidate strength is measured

Start with the senator’s two-party vote share six years earlier. Adjust for the state’s presidential lean relative to the nation and the typical Senate result that year. Reverse the sign for Republicans so that higher always means the candidate ran ahead of their party.

The main comparison calls a returning senator a standout at +15 points. Of 423 reelections, 72 qualify. A separate check uses the newspaper endorsement advantage of one party’s Senate nominee over the other. It captures both candidates’ reputations and can include first-time nominees.

How the models compare elections

The prior-performance models adjust for state, election year and the senator’s party. The House model also accounts for district presidential lean and House incumbency, giving each Senate election equal total weight. The governor model accounts for earlier state presidential lean and governor incumbency. The intervals allow for correlation within states.

Separate state and year controls do not remove state-specific changes in party strength. In presidential years, the original House and Senate baselines can also incorporate same-election presidential votes: if the senator helped that ticket too, adjustment could absorb some spillover. The history check above instead uses a baseline observed before reelection.

For Senate, the outcome is the senator’s next performance against the benchmark. For House and governor, it is the party’s two-party vote share. The latter combines persuasion and turnout. It does not tell us which voters changed their minds or turned out because of a candidate.

Measuring the whole effect: turnout and persuasion

The historical models above use vote shares. A total-effect study should also measure the other nominees’ votes per pre-election eligible voter and their net vote margin over the opposing party per eligible voter. Reporting turnout, vote share and ballot completion alongside those outcomes helps interpret changes. Aggregate returns alone cannot uniquely separate voters changing sides from changes in who votes.

Turnout, campaign spending and mobilization during the campaign are possible pathways through which candidate strength helps or hurts teammates. Conditioning on them would change the question away from the total candidacy effect. A single-ad experiment may itself affect both turnout and persuasion, but measures the ad’s incremental effect, not the whole candidate.

Does the endorsement result test exceptional campaign talent?

The DeLuca, Moskowitz and Schneer study finds near-zero average spillovers after accounting for correlated candidate quality across races. Its authors explicitly distinguish governing quality from campaigning quality: the endorsement measure mainly targets the former, while also correlating with campaign skills. This is relevant evidence, but an incomplete measure of unusually compelling campaigners. The pre-election campaign ratings described above target that construct more directly. Read the study ↗

Who appears in each sample?

The main Senate and House models cover 423 reelections across 49 states and 3,175 contested House races during 1982–2022. The governor model uses 196 concurrent races across 43 states during 1982–2020. Its sample is selected separately. See the explorer for coverage of individual elections.

A returning-incumbent sample excludes candidates who lost their first race or chose not to run again. Its bottom decile starts around three points below benchmark, and no included senator had previously run 15 points below it. A historically weak incumbent in this sample may still have been an effective candidate.

How much do the modeling choices matter?

Comparing the 15-point standouts across states in the same party and year gives a +1.4-point House estimate. Accounting for persistent differences within each state and party gives −1.4 points. Both intervals include zero. That sensitivity is a reason to be cautious about the sign of the small House estimate.

Governor estimates are less precise. Adding the governor candidates’ endorsement score to the continuous model reduces the sample from 196 to 103 elections. Small samples also limit how much we can learn about any one unusually strong or weak candidate.

Why use past performance instead of future fame?

A past race tells us something voters had already revealed when the senator stood for reelection. A future presidency selects only a handful of national winners and misses politicians with strong local appeal. Unique donor participation is a promising complementary measure. The prediction tests above evaluate the available fundraising measures. A complete in-state donor census and a separate strategy for identifying coattails are still needed.

First-time candidates need a different test. One approach is to compare pre-primary polls of several potential nominees facing the same opponent, then study close primaries where the higher-rated nominee barely won or lost. As a historical example, Senate candidate Barack Obama headlined a fundraiser that brought in $75,000 for Illinois House candidate Melissa Bean in 2004. That documents one way to help a teammate, while leaving the change in votes uncertain. Contemporary reporting ↗
